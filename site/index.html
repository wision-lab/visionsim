Unified perception requires diverse sensing

The same room and the same instant can be perceived differently depending on the sensor. Which one is right?

A single exposure is a compromise

A conventional camera picks one exposure for the whole frame, and this room makes that choice hard: the lamp lands about four stops brighter than the unlit corners. The camera has to re-meter as the lamp comes on: expose for the table and the lamp burns out, expose for the lamp and the table is grain.

One bit per pixel captures the highlights and shadows

A single-photon camera either detects a photon in a pixel or it does not, so each frame carries one bit per pixel and looks like noise on its own. Sum eight frames and the room starts to appear. Sum more, invert the camera's response function, and both the unlit table and the lit lampshade come back.

When sparse updates saturate the bandwidth

An event camera instead transmits only changes: each pixel reports when the light falling on it changes by more than a threshold, so a still room produces almost nothing and the sensor is quick on motion and edges. When the lamp switches on, that change happens everywhere at once and the whole array floods in a single instant.

Seeing through darkness and through time

A thermal camera records long-wave infrared radiance rather than reflected light, so switching the lamp on does not immediately illuminate the scene for it. That also means it keeps a kind of record: the floor stays about 0.2 K warmer where a foot landed, seconds after the robot has moved on.

Every annotation, for every view.

A single scene is rendered once with dense, pixel-perfect, temporally synchronised ground truth, so any downstream task learns from exact labels.

Batteries included.

It comes with a large-scale dataset rendered through this pipeline: 100M raw data points from eight sensors, across 100 indoor scenes, with 480k synchronized ground-truth views. Coming soon. The prerelease is available in the meantime.
